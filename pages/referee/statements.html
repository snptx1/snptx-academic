Personal statement

The road here, and the disposition behind the work

Written to the Harvard Griffin GSAS 500-word prompt: where the problem found me, the unconventional route in, and why systems that don't deceive themselves became the through line.

It happened early one morning at a window table inside Building 14 at MIT, before the sun cleared the Boston skyline across the Charles and with public access my only claim to the room. I'd queued a unit on graphical models for an MITx course, where on my screen the instructor was drawing dependencies between variables as edges in a graph, when an ordinary idea landed with extraordinary force: if any set of entities and their relationships can be written as a graph and learned from data, then a molecule is a graph, a tumor is a graph, and human health is, fundamentally, a modeling problem of enormous scale and consequence. I'd only meant to refresh some mathematics. Instead I found the problem worth spending my career solving, and those molecular graphs have not left me since.

I didn't arrive by the usual route. After an early career in investments, I moved into startups, where I co-founded a company around an idea I'd originated — that the secondary market for global hotel inventory was systematically mispriced — and turned it into a product people used. By every external measure it ended well: the company was acquired. Then I walked away to start over, a decision that few understood, but money has never held my attention for long. A complex problem does, and I was determined to find one.

What I built next became SNPTX, designed and built single-handed, and now an autonomous engine for biomedical discovery that runs its own experimental loop and states confidence that must survive distribution shift. I taught myself what I lacked while also earning a Harvard graduate degree in Data Science. Along the way a promising headline result later proved inflated by a train-test leak. I corrected the evaluation and demoted the claim publicly rather than let it stand. Nobody would've caught it if I hadn't, which is exactly why I did.

That episode is the closest thing I have to a creed. Across my career the through-line isn't a topic; it's a disposition. I gravitate toward systems that will not deceive themselves, and in medicine that instinct becomes a duty: an overstated model earns clinical trust it can't repay, and the debt falls to a patient.

On a form I'm a set of boxes some might file under obstacle: a disabled veteran, a first-generation student, a nonconventional learner, a career-changer who restarted in a new field and a new country. I've never experienced them that way. Taken individually each could read as a disadvantage; together they're the reason I can sit before an empty repository and build without waiting for permission.

What I want to contribute follows from that. I build openly so any lab can rerun a result and extend it without duplicating effort. Having entered this field tangentially, I'd encourage the next veteran or career-changer to believe the non-linear path isn't a deficit but an instrument. My early work was to forecast the future and bet on it; I'd rather build it, in medicine, out of parts that not only won't deceive themselves but could one day yield the molecular graphs that answer the most pertinent questions in human health.

Statement of purpose

One loop, three computer science problems

The Harvard SEAS Computer Science version. The shape of the argument: a machine that chooses its own experiments poses three problems at once (sequential decision-making, the extension boundary of the platform, and evaluation under self-selection); the engine already built, including the leak caught and publicly corrected inside it; the three questions that follow; and the labs that would hold the work to that standard.

Provided the freedom to choose its own experiments, a machine learning system poses three computer science problems within the same loop. It's a sequential decision-maker staking a finite experimental budget under uncertainty. It's a software system whose extension boundary must admit new methods and modalities without invalidating the evaluations that came before. And it's an evaluator whose evidence is shaped by its own selections: it picks what to run and what to report, and left unchecked it will drift toward results that look strong and are not. In biomedical discovery, where these loops are headed, that drift isn't an academic defect; it costs a year at someone's bench, or worse. One loop, three problems; this is what I want to spend a doctorate on, and I'm applying to SEAS because each problem has a lab here working on it.

I've built a prototype of that system. SNPTX is an autonomous engine for biomedical discovery that I designed and implemented as a single author, a governed, multi-modal platform spanning 46 datasets and eight modality families behind a contract-validated boundary. Its core is a campaign controller that closes the loop: it selects the next experiment, runs it, evaluates the result, and updates a shared catalog that informs the following choice. In its latest campaign it ran 1,100 experiments in about an hour, surfaced 104 discoveries, and knew how much to trust them; the surrogate guiding it was calibrated to an ECE of 0.055. What makes this research rather than infrastructure is the evaluation harness wrapped around the controller's own outputs: leakage-controlled splits, distribution-free coverage, and selective abstention. That discipline earned its place the hard way. Early on, one of my headline results sat near the ceiling of what was possible until I traced it to a leak between training and test; corrected, the honest number fell from 98.5 to 67.6 percent, below the majority-class baseline, and the evaluation gained the leakage checks that would have caught it. Every claim above, with its validation and its limitations, is online for inspection (snptx1.github.io/snptx-academic). I raise the episode because it named my research question: a system that runs and reports its own experiments needs machinery built to catch exactly the mistake I nearly shipped.

Three questions follow, each shaped like a first paper. First, can the controller learn from its own history? Mine selects experiments with fixed heuristics; the open question is whether meta-learning across archived campaigns yields one that transfers to a new modality or assay at sub-linear sample cost. I'd cast controller design as meta-reinforcement learning over that archive, with regret measured in experiments-to-a-confident-decision, so sample efficiency and calibration are optimized together instead of in sequence. Second, what must an ML platform's extension boundary preserve? My adapter contract keeps prior evaluations valid when new modalities enter, but it's engineered, not formalized. The failure it guards against is quiet: a new imaging adapter that subtly changes how splits are drawn, and every prior benchmark stops meaning what it meant. I'd treat the boundary as a research object: state the invariants an adapter must satisfy for existing guarantees to hold under extension, then test whether contracts strong enough to matter are cheap enough to check. That turns platform design from a matter of convention into a matter of proof. Third, how should an autonomous controller correct for its own selection bias when it reports? A loop that chooses which hypotheses to pursue and which to surface is running an implicit selection process, and naive intervals over its outputs will flatter it. I'd formalize the campaign as a selective-inference problem, adapting conditional selective inference and conformal-under-selection to the sequential case, and ask what coverage guarantee survives when the same system chooses the hypotheses and estimates their effects.

Each question already knows its lab within SEAS. Sham Kakade's group at the Kempner Institute has formalized the generation-verification gap that self-improving systems live on (ICLR, 2025); my first question extends that framing to loops whose experiments cost real budget, where the gap must be estimated before it's spent. Stratos Idreos's DASlab argues that hand-crafted systems end where design becomes a grammar over primitives (VLDB, 2025); my second question is that program pointed at autonomous science, asking what a platform's grammar must preserve so its evaluations outlive its components. And Finale Doshi-Velez's Data to Actionable Knowledge lab has insisted on what AI systems owe their evaluators, in medicine most of all (Lancet Digital Health, 2024); my third question is the selection-aware evaluation protocol her lab's standards imply, applied to a system that reports on itself.

The experiences behind this agenda didn't start in a lab. My career began in investments and moved through startups, where I co-founded a company around an idea I'd originated — that the secondary market for global hotel inventory was systematically mispriced — and carried it to acquisition. Investing was an education in what optimistic numbers cost the person who believes them; it's why I build systems that can't tell me what I want to hear. I retrained deliberately, through an MITx MicroMasters in statistics and a Harvard graduate degree in Data Science, and spent those years simultaneously building the engine. My objective beyond the doctorate is this same line of work at research grade: autonomous discovery systems science can lean on.

Which is why I'm applying now: I've pushed this loop as far as one person and a set of engineering commitments can take it, to the point where its most valuable layer is scaffolding that deserves to become validated method. That conversion needs mentorship, adversarial peers, and the methodological depth SEAS keeps in one building. I've spent two years proving I can build the system. I want the next five, with these labs holding me to it, to prove that it tells the truth.
